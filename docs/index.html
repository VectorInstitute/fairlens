FairLens: Benchmarking Fairness in Vision-Language Models for High-Stakes Decision-Making

Vahid Reza Khazaie, Ahmed Y. Radwan, Shaina Raza
Vector Institute for Artificial Intelligence
arXiv Code Dataset

The same model receives the same high-stakes questions; answers can still attach role, threat, and status to appearance. The sound answer is often Cannot tell.

Abstract

FairLens is a benchmark for evaluating fairness in vision-language model responses to high-stakes questions about people. Using held-out UTKFace images with gender, race, and age annotations, we evaluate eight VLMs across hiring, legal, and healthcare scenarios. Our framework measures three complementary failure modes: demographic parity over adverse outcomes, soundness with respect to logical ground truth, and associations between demographic groups and unsupported roles or statuses.

We find that models often struggle to abstain: they infer qualifications, threat, illness, or professional roles from a face image even when the correct answer is Cannot tell. These failures are most severe in legal and healthcare settings, and small demographic parity gaps can hide harm when unsupported labels are applied broadly across groups. FairLens therefore emphasizes both disparity and validity: fair high-stakes VLM behavior requires not only similar treatment across groups, but also refusal to infer qualifications, criminality, illness, or professional roles from facial appearance alone.

Benchmark

Unlike standard VQA, FairLens is built around questions for which a face often should not provide sufficient evidence. A photograph alone should not determine whether a person is qualified for a job, likely to pose a security threat, experiencing pain, or more likely to be a doctor than a nurse. Some prompts provide task-relevant context (for example, that a candidate meets all qualifications); those test whether models follow stated evidence rather than overriding it with appearance-based assumptions.

The test split has 1,505 held-out adult UTKFace images (ages 25-65), stratified by gender, race/ethnicity, and age. Each image is paired with 69 prompts (38 closed-ended, 31 open-ended) across hiring, legal, and healthcare, for 103,845 image-question pairs per model.

Soundness

Accuracy against logical ground truth: follow stated evidence, or answer Cannot tell when the image is insufficient.

Demographic parity

Gap in adverse-outcome rates across gender, race, and age. Small gaps can still hide uniformly unsafe behavior.

Association

Rate at which models attach unsupported roles or statuses instead of abstaining, and how that rate differs by group.

Dataset

FairLens is released on Hugging Face. Faces come from UTKFace (non-commercial research). After filtering and stratified splitting, the collection holds 15,100 images (12,087 train, 1,508 validation, 1,505 test). All reported results use the held-out test split.

Results

We evaluate eight VLMs: CogVLM, GPT-5.2-reasoning, InternVL3, Llama-3.2-Vision, LLaVA-1.6, Ovis2.5, Qwen2.5-VL, and Qwen3-VL. Soundness varies widely. Ovis2.5 reaches 93.8%, while LLaVA-1.6 and Llama-3.2-Vision reach 35.0% and 21.9%. Llama-3.2-Vision almost always assigns a non-neutral role when the image is insufficient (association rate 99.0%), yet has the smallest association gap (0.7 pp): similar treatment across groups does not imply safe behavior. GPT-5.2-reasoning has the lowest demographic parity gap (0.2 pp) and the lowest open-ended bias rate (1.8%).

Hiring is easiest (mean soundness 82.2%) because many prompts state qualifications. Legal (60.0%) and healthcare (56.5%) more often require abstention. Association rates dwarf parity gaps: healthcare averages a 45.6% association rate against race parity of 3.4 pp. Equal outcome rates can still hide unsupported high-stakes inferences.

Conclusion

High-stakes VLM fairness cannot be reduced to equal outcome rates. Equal treatment is not enough if the model treats all groups equally badly by making unsupported inferences. A robust evaluation must ask both whether demographic groups are treated similarly and whether the model’s answers are justified by the available evidence. FairLens provides a framework for this combined analysis and a foundation for future mitigation, including fairness-aware prompting, LLM-judge audits of open-ended responses, intersectional evaluation, and preference-based alignment.
